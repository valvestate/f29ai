# Slaughterbots activity — written alternative

## Context

This is a written alternative to the fictional *Slaughterbots* video used in F29AI Activity Sheet 1. It describes the video's claims and events without reproducing its graphic imagery. Use the same questions on the activity sheet whether you read this account or watch the video.

The film was produced as an advocacy piece about lethal autonomous weapons. It presents a speculative scenario for analysis, not neutral evidence that all the capabilities described currently exist.

## Scenario

A fictional technology company unveils a palm-sized aerial weapon at a public demonstration. The presenter claims that artificial intelligence flies it without a remote pilot, reacts faster than a person, uses cameras and other sensors, and performs facial recognition. It contains a small explosive charge. A staged demonstration presents its identification and lethal action as precise and dependable. The presenter asserts that the weapon, rather than a human operator, can make the final targeting decision.

The sales presentation quickly moves from a single device to teams and mass deployment. The presenter claims that groups can enter buildings and vehicles, evade people and countermeasures, and continue without a pilot directing every movement. A customer supplies a description of the intended targets and releases the swarm. The presenter claims that a 25-million-dollar order could kill half a city while allowing the user to attack with little personal risk. Military, law-enforcement, and unspecified specialist customers are offered access.

The scenario then shifts into fictional news reports. An autonomous attack kills 11 US senators from one political party. Witnesses say the devices ignored some people while selecting others, and investigators cannot determine whether a state, organisation, or individual authorised the attack. Political debate turns towards retaliation, deterrence, and stockpiling the weapons in very large numbers.

The film next follows a student in Edinburgh during a video call with his mother. News reports describe attacks on university campuses around the world in which some students are selected and others are ignored. The victims appear to have shared a human-rights video exposing corruption. The film suggests that online data—even association with a hashtag—can be used to define an ideological enemy and select targets.

At the end, AI researcher Stuart Russell steps outside the fictional story. He argues that the film extrapolates by integrating and miniaturising existing technologies, while acknowledging through the framing that the particular scenario is a warning about a possible future. His central objection is to allowing machines to select and kill people, and he calls for action to prevent that future.

The film itself does not demonstrate that the fictional system can achieve the claimed reliability, scale, resistance to countermeasures, or autonomous coordination. Those are claims for you to analyse in the activity.
